# SEGMENT 1 — THE BIG PICTURE
*Questions 49, 45, 17, 37, 32, 18, 31*

### Q49 — What are Large Language Models?

**AARAV:** Simplest question first. What is an LLM?

**MEERA:** An LLM is an AI system trained on a huge amount of text, so it can understand and generate human-like language. During training it learns patterns, context, and relationships between words and ideas. That lets it answer questions, summarise, translate, write code, and write creatively.

**AARAV:** And "large" means...?

**MEERA:** Two things: a large number of parameters, which are the learned numbers inside the model, often billions, and a large amount of training data. Think of someone who has read a huge library and learned how language fits together, as opposed to someone who just memorised a few books.

**AARAV:** *[laughs]* So I shouldn't say "it's like a smart autocomplete"?

**MEERA:** You can say it, but add that it predicts the next token using context learned at scale. That's what makes it useful, not just fancy autocomplete.

> **Interview one-liner:** "An LLM is a very large neural network trained on massive text data to predict and generate language, which lets it handle many tasks without building a separate model for each."

---

### Q45 — How are LLMs different from traditional statistical language models?

**AARAV:** People always compare LLMs with the older approaches. What are the main differences?

**MEERA:** Four things.

**MEERA:** *Architecture.* LLMs use transformers with self-attention, so they can connect words that are far apart in a sentence. Older models like N-grams only look at a few previous words, and HMMs, Hidden Markov Models, struggle with long-range links.

**MEERA:** *Scale.* LLMs have billions of parameters and train on massive datasets. Traditional models are small and usually built for one task.

**MEERA:** *Training.* LLMs go through large-scale pre-training on unlabelled text, then fine-tuning. Traditional models usually need labelled data for each task.

**MEERA:** *Context.* LLMs create contextual embeddings: the word "bank" gets a different representation in "river bank" and "bank account." Older methods used static embeddings, one fixed vector per word.

**AARAV:** So the big win is context.

**MEERA:** Context and scale together. A bigram model can't understand "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because *it* was too big," because it only sees the previous word. An LLM can look at the whole sentence.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Traditional models are small, task-specific, and see limited context. LLMs are huge, pre-trained on general text, and use attention to understand context across the whole input."

---

### Q17 — What are Generative and Discriminative models?

**AARAV:** Next, a classic. Generative versus discriminative.

**MEERA:** A **discriminative** model learns the boundary between categories. It answers: "given this input, which class is it?" Mathematically, it models the *conditional* probability of the output given the input. A sentiment classifier is a good example: you give it a review, and it says positive or negative.

**MEERA:** A **generative** model learns how the data itself is distributed, so it can *create* new samples. It models the *joint* probability of inputs and outputs. A language model that predicts the next word is generative.

**AARAV:** Is there an everyday way to remember it?

**MEERA:** Imagine learning to tell cats from dogs. A discriminative learner just memorises what separates them, like ear shape. A generative learner learns what cats and dogs look like well enough to draw a new one.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Discriminative models learn the decision boundary, P of y given x. Generative models learn how the data is produced, the joint distribution, so they can create new data."

---

### Q37 — What is the difference between Discriminative AI and Generative AI?

**AARAV:** That sounds like the previous question. Is it the same thing?

**MEERA:** It's the same idea, applied to AI products. Interviewers like asking it in both forms. **Discriminative, or predictive, AI** classifies or predicts from existing data. Examples are spam detection, image recognition, and regression for things like price prediction. **Generative AI** produces new content, such as text, images, music, or code. Typical techniques are GANs, VAEs, and GPT-style transformers.

**AARAV:** So a spam filter is discriminative and a chatbot that writes the email is generative.

**MEERA:** Exactly. One decides, the other creates.

> **Interview tip:** If you get both Q17 and Q37 in one interview, give the same core idea and just change the examples. It shows you understand the concept rather than the wording.

---

### Q32 — What are the different types of Foundation Models?

**AARAV:** The PDF lists "foundation models." What does that term mean?

**MEERA:** Foundation models are large AI models trained on vast amounts of mostly unlabelled data, so they learn general-purpose knowledge that can be adapted to many tasks. You build on top of them, which is why they're called a foundation.

**MEERA:** The PDF groups them into four types.
- **Language models:** translation, summarisation, question answering. BERT and GPT-3 are examples.
- **Computer vision models:** image classification, object detection, segmentation. ResNet and VGGNet.
- **Generative models:** creative writing, image generation, music. DALL-E and Imagen.
- **Multimodal models:** work across more than one kind of data, like image captioning and visual question answering.

**AARAV:** The PDF also lists PaLM and LaMDA under multimodal.

**MEERA:** *Quick correction.* PaLM and LaMDA are primarily language models. Multimodal examples are better illustrated by models that natively handle text and images together, such as GPT-4-class and Gemini-class models. In an interview, use those as your multimodal examples.

> **Interview one-liner:** "A foundation model is a large model pre-trained on broad data that can be adapted, through prompting or fine-tuning, to many downstream tasks."

---

### Q18 — How is GPT-4 different from GPT-3?

**AARAV:** Everyone asks about GPT-4 versus GPT-3.

**MEERA:** Let me give you the genuine differences, then I'll flag the numbers. GPT-4 is a **multimodal** model: it can take images as well as text, while GPT-3 was text-only. It is **more accurate and more reliable** on reasoning tasks, follows instructions better, produces fewer harmful outputs, and has **stronger multilingual performance**. It also supports a **much larger context window**.

**AARAV:** The PDF says GPT-4 has around one trillion parameters, a 25,000-token context window, and 26 languages.

**MEERA:** *Quick correction.* This is where people get caught out. OpenAI has **not publicly disclosed** GPT-4's parameter count, so "one trillion" is a rumour, not a fact. GPT-3's 175 billion *is* official. On context, GPT-4 launched with 8,000 and 32,000-token versions, and later variants went much higher. And the "26 languages" figure comes from a language-benchmark comparison, not a hard limit on supported languages.

**AARAV:** So what should I say in an interview?

**MEERA:** Say "GPT-4 added multimodality, better reasoning and instruction following, safer behaviour, and a longer context window." If you mention parameters, say "the exact number isn't public."

> **Interview tip:** Saying "that detail isn't publicly disclosed" sounds confident and honest. Quoting an unverified number sounds risky.

---

### Q31 — How does Gemini's architecture optimise training compared to other multimodal LLMs like GPT-4?

**AARAV:** And the next one is about Gemini.

**MEERA:** The idea the PDF gives is that Gemini is designed as a **unified multimodal model**: instead of bolting an image module onto a text model, it processes text and images (and more) in a single model. That brings four benefits, according to the answer. One, **parameter sharing** across modalities, which reduces complexity. Two, **cross-modality attention**, so text and images inform each other during learning. Three, **data-efficient pre-training** using self-supervised and contrastive-style learning. Four, **balanced objectives** across modalities, which helps training stay stable.

**AARAV:** Is that all confirmed?

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* The broad idea, a **natively multimodal** design trained across modalities from the start, is well established. But detailed claims comparing training stability against GPT-4 aren't things either company has fully published. So explain the concept, native versus bolted-on multimodality, and avoid sounding certain about internal training details.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Gemini is built to be natively multimodal, so one model learns from text, images, and other inputs together, rather than stitching separate models together afterwards."

# SEGMENT 2 — TOKENS, EMBEDDINGS AND CONTEXT
*Questions 1, 14, 8, 46*

### Q1 — What is tokenization, and why is it important in LLMs?

**AARAV:** Let's start where every LLM starts, with tokens.

**MEERA:** LLMs don't read raw text. They read numbers. **Tokenization** is the step that splits text into smaller pieces called tokens, which can be whole words, parts of words, or even single characters. Each token is mapped to a number, and the model works on those numbers.

**AARAV:** Example?

**MEERA:** The word "tokenization" might become "token" plus "ization." Common words are often one token, while rare words are split into pieces.

**AARAV:** Why not just use whole words?

**MEERA:** Because the vocabulary would be enormous, and any new word would break the model. Splitting into sub-words keeps the vocabulary manageable, handles many languages, and copes with rare words. That improves both efficiency and performance.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Tokenization converts text into sub-word units and then into IDs, so the model can process any text with a manageable vocabulary."

---

### Q14 — How do LLMs handle out-of-vocabulary words?

**AARAV:** That leads straight to out-of-vocabulary words, words the model has never seen.

**MEERA:** In older systems, an unknown word was replaced by a special "unknown" symbol, and the meaning was lost. Modern LLMs avoid this with **sub-word tokenization**, using methods like **Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)** and **WordPiece**. An unfamiliar word is broken into smaller known pieces.

**AARAV:** Show me.

**MEERA:** "Unhappiness" might become "un," "happi," and "ness." The model has seen all three pieces before, so it can work out the meaning of the new word from its parts. And in the worst case, byte-level tokenizers can fall back to individual bytes, so nothing is truly unknown.

> **Interview one-liner:** "There's effectively no out-of-vocabulary problem, because rare words are broken into known sub-word pieces, and byte-level methods can represent any text."

---

### Q8 — What role do embeddings play in LLMs, and how are they initialised?

**AARAV:** Once we have token IDs, what happens next?

**MEERA:** Each ID is looked up in an **embedding table** and turned into a dense vector, a list of numbers. These vectors capture meaning, so similar words end up close together in that high-dimensional space. That's the form neural networks can work with.

**AARAV:** And how are embeddings initialised?

**MEERA:** The PDF says randomly, or with pretrained vectors like Word2Vec or GloVe. During training they're fine-tuned, meaning adjusted so they capture what's useful for the task.

**AARAV:** Is that how modern LLMs do it?

**MEERA:** *Quick correction.* Today's large language models typically **initialise embeddings randomly and learn them together with the rest of the model**. Using Word2Vec or GloVe was more common in older NLP pipelines. A good interview answer: "random initialisation, trained end-to-end, though pretrained embeddings can be used to give smaller models a head start."

> **Interview one-liner:** "Embeddings turn tokens into dense vectors that encode meaning, and they're learned during training."

---

### Q46 — What is a context window?

**AARAV:** Everyone talks about "bigger context windows." What is it?

**MEERA:** The context window is how much text, measured in tokens, the model can look at in one go, both your input *and* its own output so far. If a conversation goes past that limit, older parts are cut off or must be summarised.

**AARAV:** So why not make it infinite?

**MEERA:** Because attention costs grow quickly with length. A larger window lets the model use more surrounding information and stay coherent over long documents, but it needs more memory and computation, so it's slower and more expensive. That's the trade-off between performance and resource efficiency.

**AARAV:** Handy way to picture it?

**MEERA:** A desk. The context window is how many papers fit on the desk at once. Anything not on the desk, the model can't see.

> **Interview one-liner:** "The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can attend to at once, and larger windows help with long inputs but cost more compute and memory."

# SEGMENT 3 — INSIDE THE TRANSFORMER
*Questions 6, 15, 44, 19, 35*

### Q6 — What are Sequence-to-Sequence models?

**AARAV:** Before transformers, there were sequence-to-sequence models. What are they?

**MEERA:** A **Seq2Seq** model turns one sequence into another sequence, where the lengths can differ. Machine translation is the classic example: an English sentence comes in, a Hindi sentence comes out. Summarisation and speech recognition also fit.

**AARAV:** How do they work inside?

**MEERA:** Traditionally there are two parts: an **encoder** that reads the input and compresses it, and a **decoder** that produces the output step by step. The early versions used RNNs or LSTMs for both.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Seq2Seq maps an input sequence to an output sequence of a possibly different length, typically with an encoder-decoder design."

---

### Q15 — How does the Transformer overcome the challenges of traditional Seq2Seq models?

**AARAV:** So what was wrong with the old Seq2Seq models?

**MEERA:** Mainly that they were **slow** and **forgetful**. RNN-based models read one word at a time, so you couldn't parallelise training. And squeezing a whole sentence into one fixed-size summary created a **context bottleneck**, so long sentences lost information.

**MEERA:** Transformers fix this in several ways.
- **Parallelisation:** self-attention processes all tokens at the same time, so training and inference are much faster on GPUs.
- **Long-range dependencies:** any token can directly attend to any other token, however far apart.
- **Positional encoding:** since everything is processed together, extra position information tells the model the word order.
- **Scalability:** they scale better to large datasets and long sequences.
- **No single bottleneck:** the decoder can look at *all* the encoder outputs, not just one summary vector.

**AARAV:** So: fast, direct connections, and no squeezing.

**MEERA:** Perfect summary.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Transformers replace sequential recurrence with attention, which gives parallel training, direct long-range connections, and no fixed-size bottleneck."

---

### Q44 — How is the encoder different from the decoder?

**AARAV:** You mentioned encoder and decoder. How do they differ?

**MEERA:** The **encoder** reads the input and builds rich internal representations. It's about *understanding*. The **decoder** takes those representations plus what it has already generated, and produces the output one token at a time. It's about *producing*.

**AARAV:** Does every model have both?

**MEERA:** No, and this is a great interview extra. **Encoder-only** models like BERT are good for understanding tasks such as classification. **Decoder-only** models like the GPT family are used for generation, and this is what most modern chat LLMs are. **Encoder-decoder** models like T5 suit translation and summarisation.

> **Interview one-liner:** "The encoder understands the input, the decoder generates the output, and many modern LLMs use only the decoder."

---

### Q19 — What are positional encodings?

**AARAV:** You said transformers need position information. Why?

**MEERA:** Because self-attention looks at all tokens at once and, on its own, has no sense of order. "Dog bites man" and "Man bites dog" would look identical as a bag of words. **Positional encodings** inject the order.

**AARAV:** How?

**MEERA:** In the original transformer, a position signal is *added* to each word embedding. It's generated with **sine and cosine functions** of different frequencies, based on the position and the dimension. That gives each position a unique pattern the model can learn to use.

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* Many modern LLMs use other schemes, like **rotary position embeddings (RoPE)** or learned positions. If you mention that sinusoidal encoding is the original approach and that newer methods exist, you'll stand out.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Positional encodings give the model word-order information that self-attention doesn't have by itself."

---

### Q35 — How does Mixture of Experts improve LLM scalability?

**AARAV:** Last one in this segment: Mixture of Experts, or MoE.

**MEERA:** Imagine a hospital. You don't send every patient to every doctor. A receptionist sends you to the right specialist. In an **MoE model**, the network has many "expert" sub-networks, and a **gating function**, the receptionist, picks only a few experts for each token.

**AARAV:** And the benefit?

**MEERA:** Two big ones. **Lower compute per token**, because only a small part of the model is active at a time. And **more total capacity**: you can build a model with a huge number of parameters without paying the full cost to run all of them every time. So you scale up knowledge while keeping speed and cost under control.

**AARAV:** Any catch?

**MEERA:** Yes. You still need memory to hold all the experts, and the gate has to balance the load so some experts aren't overused. Good to mention.

> **Interview one-liner:** "MoE activates only a few specialised experts per token, so the model can have far more parameters without a proportional increase in compute."

# SEGMENT 4 — ATTENTION, UP CLOSE
*Questions 48, 30, 22, 20, 21*

### Q48 — Can you explain attention mechanisms in transformer models?

**AARAV:** Attention is the heart of all this. Explain it simply.

**MEERA:** Attention lets the model decide which parts of the input matter most for the word it's currently working on, regardless of position. Rather than treating every token equally, it learns to "pay attention" to the relevant ones.

**AARAV:** An example would help.

**MEERA:** "The dog chased the ball because it was fast." What does "it" refer to? Attention helps the model connect "it" with the right earlier word using context. The PDF says "the ball," though honestly a human might say "the dog." That ambiguity is exactly why context matters, and why this is a great sentence to use in an interview.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Attention computes how relevant each token is to every other token, and uses those weights to build context-aware representations."

---

### Q30 — How do you compute attention scores, and what is the mathematical interpretation?

**AARAV:** Now the formula everyone must know.

**MEERA:** In words: **attention equals softmax of Q times K-transpose divided by the square root of d-k, then multiplied by V.** *(Show notes: Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d_k) · V)*

**MEERA:** Here's the intuition. Every token produces three vectors: a **Query** (what am I looking for?), a **Key** (what do I contain?), and a **Value** (what information do I give?). Multiplying queries with keys, the dot product, measures similarity. We **divide by √d_k** so the numbers don't get too large, which keeps the softmax and gradients stable. **Softmax** turns the scores into weights that add up to one. Finally, we use those weights to take a weighted mix of the values.

**AARAV:** A library analogy?

**MEERA:** Your query is what you ask the librarian. The keys are labels on the books. Scores say which labels match. The values are the contents you actually read, weighted by how well they matched.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Scaled dot-product attention scores query-key similarity, normalises with softmax, and uses the weights to combine the values."

---

### Q22 — How is the dot product used in self-attention, and what are the efficiency implications?

**AARAV:** Same formula, but this time the question is about efficiency.

**MEERA:** The dot product measures how aligned a query is with a key: the larger it is, the more relevant that token. The cost issue is that every token is compared with every other token. For a sequence of length *n*, that's about *n squared* comparisons, **quadratic complexity**.

**AARAV:** So doubling the length...

**MEERA:** ...roughly quadruples the attention cost. That's why long contexts are expensive, and why researchers developed efficiency tricks such as sparse attention, linear approximations, and optimised implementations like FlashAttention.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Self-attention is quadratic in sequence length because every token attends to every other token, which is why long-context efficiency is an active research area."

---

### Q20 — What is multi-head attention?

**AARAV:** Why "multi-head"?

**MEERA:** One attention pass can only capture one kind of relationship at a time. With **multi-head attention**, the model runs several attention operations in parallel, each with its *own learned* projections of the queries, keys, and values. One head might track grammar, another might track who "it" refers to, another might focus on nearby words. Their outputs are then joined and combined into the final result.

**AARAV:** Like a team of reviewers.

**MEERA:** Exactly. Each reviewer reads the same text with a different focus, and then you merge their notes.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Multi-head attention runs several attention heads in parallel on different learned projections, so the model captures many types of relationships at once."

---

### Q21 — Derive the softmax function and explain its role in attention.

**AARAV:** "Derive softmax" sounds scary.

**MEERA:** It's less scary than it sounds. We have a list of raw scores, which can be any number, including negatives. We want probabilities: positive numbers that add up to one.

**MEERA:** Step one: apply the exponential function to each score, which makes everything positive and boosts the larger scores. Step two: divide each by the sum of all of them, so they add up to one. In symbols, *softmax of x-i equals e to the x-i divided by the sum over j of e to the x-j.*

**AARAV:** And its role in attention?

**MEERA:** It converts raw similarity scores into **attention weights**. The model can then say, "give 70% of focus to this token, 20% to that one, and 10% to the rest."

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* Mention that implementations subtract the maximum score first for **numerical stability**. It doesn't change the result but prevents overflow.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Softmax exponentiates and normalises scores into a probability distribution, which turns similarity scores into attention weights."

# SEGMENT 5 — THE MATHS UNDER THE HOOD
*Questions 29, 24, 25, 23, 27, 26, 28, 41*

**AARAV:** Meera, I'll admit it. The maths questions scare me the most.

**MEERA:** They shouldn't. Interviewers rarely want a long derivation. They want to see that you understand *why* each idea exists. Let's go in the order that makes the story clearest.

### Q29 — What is the chain rule, and how does it apply to gradient descent?

**AARAV:** Start with the chain rule.

**MEERA:** The chain rule is how you find the derivative of a function inside another function. If you have f of g of x, the derivative is *f-prime of g of x, times g-prime of x.* You multiply the rates of change along the chain.

**AARAV:** And in deep learning?

**MEERA:** A neural network is a long chain of functions, layer after layer, ending in a loss. To improve the model, we need to know how the loss changes when each weight changes. The chain rule lets us compute that layer by layer, working backwards from the loss. That's **backpropagation**. Gradient descent then nudges each weight a little in the direction that reduces the loss.

**AARAV:** So backprop is basically the chain rule, applied efficiently.

**MEERA:** Yes. That's a line interviewers love.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Backpropagation applies the chain rule layer by layer to get gradients of the loss for every parameter, and gradient descent uses them to update the weights."

---

### Q24 — How do you compute the gradient of the loss with respect to embeddings?

**AARAV:** Now the same idea, aimed at embeddings.

**MEERA:** Embeddings are just parameters, so they get gradients like any other weight. Using the chain rule, the gradient of the loss with respect to an embedding is *the gradient of the loss with respect to the logits, times the gradient of the logits with respect to the embedding.* In practice the signal flows back through every layer between the output and the embedding.

**AARAV:** And then?

**MEERA:** The optimiser updates that embedding vector to reduce the loss. In real frameworks like PyTorch you never write this by hand. Automatic differentiation does it. One detail to mention: for a given batch, only the embeddings of the tokens that appeared get a non-zero gradient.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Embedding gradients come from the chain rule, flowing back from the loss through the network, and are then used to update the embedding vectors."

---

### Q25 — What is the role of the Jacobian matrix in backpropagation through a transformer?

**AARAV:** Jacobian. That word sounds intimidating.

**MEERA:** A **Jacobian** is just a table of derivatives. If a layer takes a vector in and produces a vector out, the Jacobian records how *each* output number changes with respect to *each* input number.

**AARAV:** Why does backprop need it?

**MEERA:** Because layers in a transformer, like attention or feed-forward blocks, are vector-to-vector functions. The chain rule for vectors multiplies Jacobians together to carry the gradient backwards.

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* In practice, frameworks never build those giant matrices. They compute **Jacobian-vector products** efficiently, which is exactly what reverse-mode automatic differentiation does. Saying that shows real understanding.

> **Interview one-liner:** "The Jacobian describes how a layer's outputs change with its inputs, and backprop chains these local derivatives, usually as efficient vector-Jacobian products."

---

### Q23 — Explain cross-entropy loss and why it's used in language modelling.

**AARAV:** Now the loss itself. Why cross-entropy?

**MEERA:** A language model outputs a probability for every token in the vocabulary. The correct next token is known. **Cross-entropy** measures how far the predicted distribution is from the true one. With a single correct token, it simplifies to *minus the log of the probability the model gave the correct token.*

**AARAV:** So if the model gave the right word a high probability...

**MEERA:** ...the loss is small. If it gave it a tiny probability, the loss is large. So it punishes confident wrong answers heavily and pushes the model to put probability on the right token. It also pairs nicely with softmax, giving clean gradients, and it directly maximises the likelihood of the training data.

**MEERA:** *Extra credit.* **Perplexity**, a common language-model metric, is just the exponential of the average cross-entropy.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Cross-entropy is the negative log-probability of the correct token. Minimising it maximises the likelihood of the data."

---

### Q27 — How is KL divergence used in evaluating LLM outputs?

**AARAV:** KL divergence. How is that different from cross-entropy?

**MEERA:** **KL divergence** measures how one probability distribution differs from another. With P as the "true" distribution and Q as the model's, KL of P from Q is *the sum of P times the log of P over Q.* A value of zero means the distributions match, and bigger means they differ more.

**AARAV:** Where do we see it in LLMs?

**MEERA:** Cross-entropy equals the entropy of the true distribution *plus* KL divergence, so minimising cross-entropy minimises KL. It also appears in **knowledge distillation**, comparing student and teacher outputs, and in **RLHF-style fine-tuning**, where a KL penalty keeps the tuned model from drifting too far from the original.

**MEERA:** *Quick note.* It's **not symmetric**: KL of P from Q is not the same as KL of Q from P. And "lower KL means predictions closely match the targets" is right, but remember it's one tool among many, not a complete quality measure for text.

> **Interview one-liner:** "KL divergence measures how far the model's distribution is from a reference, and it's used in training, distillation, and to keep fine-tuned models close to a base model."

---

### Q26 — Eigenvalues and eigenvectors in the context of dimensionality reduction

**AARAV:** Back to linear algebra. Eigen-what?

**MEERA:** For a matrix A, an **eigenvector** is a direction that doesn't change direction when A is applied. It just gets stretched or shrunk. The stretch factor is the **eigenvalue**. The equation is *A times v equals lambda times v.*

**AARAV:** And how does that help reduce dimensions?

**MEERA:** In **PCA, Principal Component Analysis**, you take the covariance matrix of your data. Its eigenvectors are the **principal components**, the directions along which data varies most. The eigenvalues say how much variance each direction captures. You keep the components with the largest eigenvalues and drop the rest. That's how you shrink the data while preserving most of its information.

**AARAV:** Real-life picture?

**MEERA:** A flat, tilted pancake of points in 3D. Two directions capture almost everything, and the third is thin noise. PCA finds the pancake's plane.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Eigenvectors of the covariance matrix give the principal directions, eigenvalues give the variance along them, and we keep the top ones to reduce dimensions."

---

### Q28 — Derive the derivative of ReLU and discuss its significance.

**AARAV:** An easy derivation, I hope?

**MEERA:** Very easy. **ReLU**, Rectified Linear Unit, is *f of x equals max of zero and x.* If x is positive, f is just x, so the slope is **one**. If x is negative, f is zero, so the slope is **zero**.

**AARAV:** What happens exactly at zero?

**MEERA:** Technically it isn't differentiable there, and in practice frameworks just pick zero or one. It almost never matters.

**AARAV:** And why does ReLU matter?

**MEERA:** It adds **non-linearity**, which networks need to learn complex patterns. It's very **cheap** to compute. And for positive inputs the gradient is exactly one, so it **helps with vanishing gradients** compared with sigmoid or tanh. The downside is the "dying ReLU" problem: a neuron stuck in the negative region outputs zero and stops learning.

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* Many modern LLMs use smoother variants like **GELU** or **SwiGLU** in their feed-forward layers. Mentioning that shows you know current practice.

> **Interview one-liner:** "ReLU's derivative is one for positive inputs and zero for negative ones. It's cheap, adds non-linearity, and eases vanishing gradients."

---

### Q41 — What is the vanishing gradient problem, and how does the Transformer address it?

**AARAV:** You mentioned vanishing gradients. Remind me what that is.

**MEERA:** During backprop, gradients get multiplied layer after layer. If those numbers are less than one, the gradient shrinks as it travels back, and early layers barely learn. That's the **vanishing gradient problem**. It was especially bad in RNNs over long sequences.

**AARAV:** How do transformers handle it?

**MEERA:** Three main things. **Self-attention** gives short, direct paths between any two tokens, so the signal doesn't have to pass through a long chain of steps. **Residual connections**, the skip connections, let gradients flow straight through the network. **Layer normalisation** keeps activations and gradients at a stable scale, which helps against both vanishing and exploding gradients.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Transformers reduce vanishing gradients through direct attention paths, residual connections, and layer normalisation."

# SEGMENT 6 — HOW MODELS ARE TRAINED
*Questions 5, 9, 7, 47, 16, 40*

### Q5 — What is masked language modelling, and how does it help pretraining?

**AARAV:** Let's talk about pretraining. First, masked language modelling.

**MEERA:** In **masked language modelling, or MLM**, you hide some random tokens, say 15%, with a mask symbol, and train the model to predict them from the surrounding words. For example: "The cat sat on the [MASK]." The model has to guess "mat" using both the left and right context.

**AARAV:** Why is that useful?

**MEERA:** It forces the model to learn how words relate to each other, which builds a deep understanding of language before any task-specific fine-tuning. BERT is the famous example.

> **Interview one-liner:** "MLM hides tokens and trains the model to recover them from bidirectional context, building a strong understanding of language during pre-training."

---

### Q9 — What is next sentence prediction, and how is it useful?

**AARAV:** BERT also had a second training task, right?

**MEERA:** Yes, **Next Sentence Prediction, or NSP**. The model gets two sentences. Half the time, the second sentence really follows the first. The other half, it's random. The model has to classify whether they belong together. The goal was to teach sentence relationships, which matter for question answering and dialogue.

**AARAV:** Is it still used?

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* Later research, such as RoBERTa, found NSP added little, and many newer models dropped it or replaced it with other objectives. So say it was introduced in BERT to learn sentence-level relationships, and that its usefulness was later questioned.

> **Interview one-liner:** "NSP trains a binary classifier to tell whether sentence B follows sentence A. It was used in BERT, though later work showed it adds little."

---

### Q7 — How do autoregressive models differ from masked models?

**AARAV:** And now the big comparison.

**MEERA:** **Autoregressive models**, like GPT, generate text *one token at a time*, left to right, each token predicted from the previous ones. They can only look backwards. **Masked models**, like BERT, fill in hidden tokens using context from *both* sides.

**AARAV:** Which is better?

**MEERA:** Neither, it depends on the job. Autoregressive models are natural for **generation**, because generation is itself left-to-right. Masked models are strong at **understanding and classification**, because seeing both directions helps. That's why chat assistants are autoregressive, while many classifiers and search encoders use masked-style models.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Autoregressive models predict the next token from the left context, which suits generation. Masked models predict hidden tokens from both sides, which suits understanding."

---

### Q47 — What is a hyperparameter?

**AARAV:** Quick one. Hyperparameter versus parameter?

**MEERA:** **Parameters** are learned *by* the model during training, like weights. **Hyperparameters** are set *by you* before training and control how training happens. Examples: the **learning rate**, batch size, number of epochs, the choice of optimiser such as Adam, the choice of activation function, and the train-test split.

**AARAV:** Memory trick?

**MEERA:** Parameters are what the model learns. Hyperparameters are the dials you set before the model starts learning.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Parameters are learned from data. Hyperparameters are chosen beforehand and control the training process."

---

### Q16 — What is overfitting, and how can it be prevented?

**AARAV:** Everyone's favourite: overfitting.

**MEERA:** Overfitting is when a model does great on training data but poorly on new data, because it memorised noise and quirks instead of learning general patterns. It's like a student who memorises last year's answer sheet and then fails when the questions change.

**MEERA:** Fixes: **regularisation**, with L1 or L2 penalties on the loss. **Dropout**, randomly switching off neurons during training. **Data augmentation**, adding variety to the training set. **Early stopping**, halting when validation loss stops improving. And **simpler models**, with fewer parameters or layers. More data also helps.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Overfitting means high training accuracy but poor generalisation. We prevent it with regularisation, dropout, more or augmented data, early stopping, and simpler models."

---

### Q40 — How does Adaptive Softmax speed up large language models?

**AARAV:** Last one in this segment is a less common one: adaptive softmax.

**MEERA:** Remember that at the output layer, the model computes a probability for *every* word in the vocabulary. With a vocabulary of hundreds of thousands, that's expensive. **Adaptive softmax** exploits the fact that words follow a frequency pattern: a few words are very common and most are rare.

**MEERA:** It groups words into **frequency-based clusters**. Frequent words get the full, precise computation. Rare words go through smaller, cheaper computations. Since rare words are rare, you save a lot of work overall with little loss of accuracy.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Adaptive softmax groups words by frequency and spends less computation on rare ones, which speeds up large-vocabulary models."

# SEGMENT 7 — FINE-TUNING AND MAKING MODELS SMALLER
*Questions 2, 33, 12, 13*

### Q2 — What are LoRA and QLoRA?

**AARAV:** Fine-tuning a huge model sounds expensive. How do people do it on normal hardware?

**MEERA:** Using techniques like **LoRA and QLoRA**. **LoRA, Low-Rank Adaptation**, freezes the original model weights and adds small, trainable "adapter" matrices to certain layers. These are *low-rank*, meaning made of two thin matrices, so they contain only a tiny fraction of the parameters. You train only those.

**AARAV:** So the base model isn't changed?

**MEERA:** Right. The base stays frozen, which saves a huge amount of GPU memory. The PDF says LoRA adds parameters "without increasing the overall size." More precisely, the adapters are small, and they can be **merged back into the base weights** after training, so inference cost doesn't increase.

**MEERA:** **QLoRA** goes one step further: it **quantises** the frozen base model to 4-bit precision, shrinking memory use dramatically, and still trains LoRA adapters on top. It uses a special 4-bit format called **NormalFloat (NF4)**, **double quantisation** that compresses the quantisation constants too, and **paged optimisers** that handle memory spikes. The result is fine-tuning of very large models on a single GPU, with quality close to full fine-tuning.

> **Interview one-liner:** "LoRA trains small low-rank adapters on a frozen model. QLoRA does the same on a 4-bit quantised base model to cut memory even further."

---

### Q33 — How does PEFT prevent catastrophic forgetting?

**AARAV:** LoRA is a form of PEFT, right?

**MEERA:** Yes. **Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning** is the family of methods, including LoRA, adapters, and prompt or prefix tuning, that update only a small set of parameters and keep most of the model frozen. Because the original weights stay untouched, the model's core abilities are far less likely to be overwritten while it learns the new task.

**AARAV:** So it *reduces* forgetting, not eliminates it.

**MEERA:** Good catch, and worth saying in an interview. PEFT lowers the risk because the base is frozen, but the adapted model's behaviour can still shift on other tasks. And as a bonus, you can keep one base model and swap in different small adapters for different tasks.

> **Interview one-liner:** "PEFT freezes most weights and trains a small number of parameters, so the original knowledge is largely preserved."

---

### Q12 — How can catastrophic forgetting be mitigated?

**AARAV:** And what exactly is catastrophic forgetting?

**MEERA:** It's when a model learns a new task and loses what it knew about earlier ones, because the weights that mattered for the old task get overwritten. Imagine learning Spanish so intensely that you forget your French.

**MEERA:** The PDF lists three strategy families.
- **Rehearsal:** mix some old data with the new data when training, so the model keeps practising old skills.
- **Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC):** work out which weights were important for earlier tasks, and penalise changing them too much.
- **Modular approaches,** such as progressive neural networks: add new modules for new tasks and leave the old ones intact.

**AARAV:** Anything modern to add?

**MEERA:** Yes. Parameter-efficient methods like LoRA, which we just covered, and using a **low learning rate** are practical, widely used ways to limit forgetting.

> **Interview one-liner:** "We mitigate catastrophic forgetting with rehearsal, regularisation like EWC, modular architectures, and in practice PEFT with careful learning rates."

---

### Q13 — What is model distillation, and how is it applied to LLMs?

**AARAV:** And making models smaller?

**MEERA:** That's **distillation**. A large, powerful **teacher** model trains a smaller **student** model to imitate it. The key trick is that the student learns from the teacher's **soft predictions**, the full probability distributions, rather than just the single correct label.

**AARAV:** Why is that better?

**MEERA:** Because the soft outputs carry extra information. If the teacher says a picture is 70% cat, 25% lynx, and 5% dog, the student learns that cats and lynxes are similar. That "dark knowledge" helps a small model punch above its weight. The outcome: lower memory and compute, similar performance, which is ideal for phones and edge devices.

**MEERA:** *Extra credit.* With LLMs, distillation can also mean training a student on text *generated* by a stronger model.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Distillation trains a small student to mimic a large teacher's output distribution, keeping most of the performance at a fraction of the cost."

# SEGMENT 8 — HOW MODELS PICK THEIR WORDS
*Questions 3, 4, 10*

### Q3 — What is beam search, and how does it differ from greedy decoding?

**AARAV:** The model has probabilities for the next token. How does it decide what to output?

**MEERA:** The simplest way is **greedy decoding**: always pick the single most probable token at each step. It's fast, but short-sighted. A locally best word can lead to a poor sentence overall.

**MEERA:** **Beam search** keeps several candidate sequences alive at once. If the beam width is, say, 5, it tracks the top five partial sentences, expands each, and keeps the best five overall. At the end it picks the best complete sequence. So it explores more options and tends to find higher-probability sequences.

**AARAV:** The PDF says that makes the output more coherent.

**MEERA:** *Quick nuance.* For tasks like translation and summarisation, yes. For open-ended chat or creative writing, beam search can produce bland and repetitive text, which is why those use sampling methods instead, as we'll see next.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Greedy picks the best token at each step. Beam search tracks the top k sequences, so it finds better overall outputs at a higher cost."

---

### Q4 — Explain temperature in LLM text generation.

**AARAV:** Temperature. Everyone's heard of it.

**MEERA:** Temperature is a setting that controls how random the output is, by reshaping the probability distribution *before* a token is chosen. **Low temperature**, close to zero, sharpens the distribution: the model almost always picks the most probable token, so output is focused and predictable. **High temperature**, above one, flattens it, so less likely tokens get a real chance, producing more varied, creative, and sometimes less sensible text.

**AARAV:** So what's a good value?

**MEERA:** Around 0.7 is a common balance between creativity and coherence. For factual question answering or code, use lower. For brainstorming or stories, use higher.

**MEERA:** *Interview tip.* Technically, temperature divides the logits before softmax. Saying that is a nice detail. And remember: a temperature of zero is *nearly* deterministic, but that doesn't make the model correct.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Temperature scales the logits before softmax. Low means focused and deterministic, high means diverse and creative."

---

### Q10 — Top-k sampling versus nucleus (top-p) sampling

**AARAV:** And two other controls: top-k and top-p.

**MEERA:** Both are ways to sample from only the *plausible* tokens, so the model doesn't pick absurd ones.

**MEERA:** **Top-k:** keep only the k most probable tokens, say 10, and sample among them. Simple, but k is fixed regardless of the situation.

**MEERA:** **Top-p, or nucleus sampling:** keep the *smallest set* of tokens whose combined probability reaches a threshold p, like 0.9, and sample from that. The set size adapts. When the model is confident, maybe two or three tokens are enough. When it's uncertain, the set grows.

**AARAV:** So top-p adapts to context.

**MEERA:** That's the key difference, and it's usually the answer interviewers want.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Top-k uses a fixed number of candidates. Top-p uses a dynamic set based on cumulative probability, so it adapts to how confident the model is."

# SEGMENT 9 — PROMPTING AND LEARNING FROM EXAMPLES
*Questions 11, 36, 39, 42*

### Q11 — How does prompt engineering influence LLM output?

**AARAV:** Now the part everyone can try at home: prompts.

**MEERA:** LLMs are sensitive to wording. **Prompt engineering** is designing the input so the model behaves the way you want. Adding context, a clear task, a role, the output format, and constraints can noticeably improve accuracy and relevance for things like summarisation or question answering.

**AARAV:** Example?

**MEERA:** "Summarise this" gives a vague result. "You are a financial analyst. Summarise this report in three bullet points for a busy executive, focusing on risks" gives a far better one. It's especially valuable in zero-shot and few-shot settings, where you have little or no task-specific data.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Prompt engineering shapes outputs through clear instructions, context, examples, and format, with no retraining required."

---

### Q36 — What is Chain-of-Thought prompting?

**AARAV:** And the famous "think step by step."

**MEERA:** **Chain-of-Thought, CoT**, prompting asks the model to work through a problem in intermediate steps before giving the final answer. For multi-step maths, logic, or planning, this usually improves accuracy, because the model breaks the problem down instead of jumping to a guess.

**AARAV:** How do I trigger it?

**MEERA:** Either add an instruction such as "Let's think step by step," or give examples that include worked reasoning. A side benefit is more **interpretability**, since you can read the steps.

**MEERA:** *Quick caveat.* The written reasoning isn't always a faithful picture of what happens inside the model, so treat it as helpful, not as proof.

> **Interview one-liner:** "CoT prompting makes the model produce intermediate reasoning steps, which improves performance on multi-step problems."

---

### Q39 — What is zero-shot learning, and how does it apply to LLMs?

**AARAV:** Zero-shot. Zero what?

**MEERA:** Zero *examples*. In zero-shot use, you give the model only an instruction, with no demonstrations, and it performs the task using what it learned in pre-training. For example, "Classify this review as positive or negative" works without any labelled training set.

**AARAV:** Why does that work?

**MEERA:** Because large pre-trained models have absorbed broad knowledge of language and tasks. That shows how well they generalise: classification, translation, and summarisation, all from just an instruction.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Zero-shot means asking the model to do a task from instructions alone, relying on knowledge from pre-training."

---

### Q42 — What is few-shot learning, and what are its advantages?

**AARAV:** And few-shot is the next step up.

**MEERA:** In **few-shot** prompting, you include a handful of examples in the prompt, perhaps two to five, so the model sees the pattern. For instance, you show three emails with the correct category, then ask it to label a new one.

**MEERA:** The advantages: **reduced data needs**, since a few examples replace a large labelled dataset. **Flexibility**, as you can switch tasks just by changing the prompt. And **cost efficiency**, because there's no new training run.

**AARAV:** Quick comparison?

**MEERA:** Zero-shot: instruction only. Few-shot: instruction plus a few examples. Fine-tuning: actually updating the model's weights. Interviewers often ask when you'd choose each, so know that ordering by effort and cost.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Few-shot learning gives the model a few in-context examples so it can perform a new task without any weight updates."

# SEGMENT 10 — GIVING MODELS KNOWLEDGE
*Questions 34, 38*

### Q34 — What are the key steps in the RAG pipeline?

**AARAV:** LLMs don't know my company's documents. How do we fix that?

**MEERA:** With **Retrieval-Augmented Generation, RAG**. Instead of relying only on what the model memorised, you fetch relevant information at question time and hand it to the model.

**MEERA:** The key steps from the PDF: **Retrieval**, where the query is converted to an embedding and compared with pre-computed document embeddings to find relevant passages. **Ranking**, where retrieved passages are ordered by relevance. **Generation**, where the top passages go into the LLM's prompt as context so it can produce a grounded answer.

**AARAV:** And before any of that happens?

**MEERA:** An offline preparation step. You split documents into chunks, create embeddings, and store them in a vector database. Without that, there's nothing to retrieve.

**MEERA:** *Why RAG matters.* It reduces hallucinations, lets you use private or up-to-date data without retraining, and allows you to cite sources.

> **Interview one-liner:** "RAG retrieves relevant documents using embeddings, ranks them, and feeds them to the LLM as context, so answers are grounded in real data."

---

### Q38 — How does knowledge graph integration enhance LLMs?

**AARAV:** And knowledge graphs?

**MEERA:** A **knowledge graph** stores facts as entities and relationships, like "Marie Curie — won — Nobel Prize in Physics." It's structured and explicit, unlike the fuzzy knowledge inside an LLM.

**MEERA:** Combining them helps in three ways. **Factual accuracy:** the model can check claims against the graph, reducing hallucinations. **Better reasoning:** the graph's relationships support multi-step questions like "Which company did the founder of X later start?" **Context understanding:** structured links give the model richer context. Common uses are question answering, entity recognition, and recommendation systems.

**AARAV:** Is this the same as RAG?

**MEERA:** Related. Retrieving from a knowledge graph to feed the model is often called **GraphRAG**, and it's especially good for questions that require connecting several facts.

> **Interview one-liner:** "Knowledge graphs give LLMs structured, verifiable facts and relationships, which improves accuracy and multi-hop reasoning."

# SEGMENT 11 — REAL-WORLD PROBLEMS
*Questions 43, 50*

### Q43 — Your LLM starts generating offensive or factually incorrect outputs. How do you diagnose and fix it?

**AARAV:** The last two are about the real world. First: the model starts saying bad things. What do you do?

**MEERA:** Interviewers love this because they want to see a *process*, not a single trick. This is how I'd structure it.

**MEERA:** **Step 1: Reproduce and analyse.** Collect the failing examples. Look for patterns: certain topics, certain prompt styles, certain users.

**MEERA:** **Step 2: Check the inputs.** Are prompts or retrieved documents causing it? Is there prompt injection, or has something changed in the system prompt?

**MEERA:** **Step 3: Look at the data.** Are there biases, gaps, or imbalances in the training or fine-tuning data? Is the preprocessing pipeline introducing errors?

**MEERA:** **Step 4: Review the model setup.** Recent fine-tuning, hyperparameters, and decoding settings like a high temperature can all cause problems.

**MEERA:** **Step 5: Fix.** Depending on the cause, that might be retraining on more balanced data, data augmentation, debiasing, adversarial training, or improving prompts. For factual errors, adding RAG often helps. Add output filters and safety classifiers as guardrails.

**MEERA:** **Step 6: Measure and monitor.** Build a test set from the failures, evaluate before and after, and keep monitoring in production.

**AARAV:** Process, then fix, then monitor.

**MEERA:** Exactly. That structure is what makes the answer sound senior.

> **Interview one-liner:** "I'd reproduce the issue, trace it to data, prompts, or model settings, apply the targeted fix, such as better data, RAG, or guardrails, and then evaluate and monitor."

### Q50 — What are common challenges of using LLMs?

**AARAV:** And finally, the challenges.

**MEERA:** Five big ones from the PDF.
- **Computational resources:** training and serving need lots of GPU power and memory.
- **Bias and fairness:** models can reproduce biases in their training data.
- **Interpretability:** it's hard to explain *why* a model produced an answer.
- **Data privacy:** large datasets raise privacy and security concerns.
- **Cost:** development, training, and deployment are expensive, which can limit smaller organisations.

**MEERA:** *Add these in an interview.* **Hallucination**, confident but wrong answers. **Prompt injection and security risks.** **Latency**, especially with long contexts. And **evaluation difficulty**, because there's often no single right answer.

> **Interview one-liner:** "The main challenges are compute and cost, bias, hallucination, interpretability, privacy and security, and the difficulty of evaluating open-ended outputs."
