HOST: Welcome, everyone. Today we are talking about large language models, the technology behind modern chat assistants, coding helpers, and a lot more. Whether you are a student, a self-learner, or a working developer, the goal is to leave with a clear mental model, not just a pile of buzzwords. I am joined by an expert who builds these systems. Welcome!
EXPERT: Thanks for having me. And I like that goal, because once the core ideas click, the rest of the field becomes much easier to follow.
HOST: Let us start at the very beginning. What exactly is a large language model?
EXPERT: A large language model, or LLM, is an artificial intelligence system trained on a huge amount of text so that it can understand, predict, and generate human-like language. While training, it picks up patterns, context, and relationships between words and ideas. That is why one model can answer questions, summarise articles, translate, write code, and even help with creative writing.
HOST: And what does the word large refer to?
EXPERT: Two things. First, the number of parameters, which are the learned numbers inside the network, often in the billions. Second, the amount of training data. Think of someone who has read an enormous library and absorbed how language works, instead of someone who memorised a few books.
HOST: How is that different from the older language models people used before?
EXPERT: There are four big differences. The first is architecture. Modern LLMs use transformers with something called self-attention, which lets them connect words that are far apart. Older models, like N-grams, only looked at a few previous words, and Hidden Markov Models struggled with long-range links.
HOST: What about size?
EXPERT: That is the second difference, scale. LLMs have billions of parameters and learn from massive datasets, so they generalise well. Traditional models were small and usually built for one specific task. Third is training. LLMs are pre-trained on large amounts of unlabelled text and then fine-tuned, while older approaches typically needed labelled data for every task.
HOST: And the fourth?
EXPERT: Context. LLMs create contextual embeddings, which means the word bank gets a different representation in river bank and bank account. Older methods used static embeddings, one fixed vector per word, so that difference was lost.
HOST: Okay. I keep hearing the terms generative and discriminative. What is the difference?
EXPERT: A discriminative model learns the boundary between categories. Given an input, it answers which class it belongs to. Mathematically, it models the probability of the output given the input. A sentiment classifier that labels a review as positive or negative is a good example.
HOST: And generative?
EXPERT: A generative model learns how the data itself is distributed, so it can create new samples. It models the joint probability of inputs and outputs. A language model that predicts the next word is generative. A simple way to remember it is this. A discriminative learner memorises what separates cats from dogs, like ear shape. A generative learner knows what cats and dogs look like well enough to draw a new one.
HOST: Is that the same as the difference between discriminative AI and generative AI as products?
EXPERT: Yes, it is the same idea applied to applications. Discriminative, or predictive, AI classifies or predicts from existing data. Think spam detection, image recognition, or predicting prices. Generative AI produces new content such as text, images, music, or code, using techniques like Generative Adversarial Networks, Variational Autoencoders, and GPT-style transformers. One decides, the other creates.
HOST: You mentioned that LLMs are adapted to many tasks. I have heard the phrase foundation model. What does it mean?
EXPERT: A foundation model is a large model trained on broad, mostly unlabelled data, so it learns general knowledge that can be adapted to many tasks. You build on top of it, which is why it is called a foundation. There are language models like BERT and GPT-3, computer vision models like ResNet and VGGNet, generative models like DALL-E and Imagen, and multimodal models that work across more than one type of data.
HOST: Multimodal meaning text plus images, for example?
EXPERT: Exactly. One small correction on a common list, though. PaLM and LaMDA are mainly language models. If you want multimodal examples, GPT-4-class and Gemini-class models are better ones, because they handle text and images together.
HOST: Speaking of GPT-4, how is it different from GPT-3?
EXPERT: GPT-4 is multimodal, so it can accept images as well as text, while GPT-3 was text only. It is more accurate on reasoning tasks, follows instructions better, produces fewer harmful outputs, performs better across many languages, and supports a much larger context window.
HOST: I have seen numbers like one trillion parameters for GPT-4.
EXPERT: Be careful there. OpenAI has not publicly disclosed GPT-4's parameter count, so one trillion is a rumour, not a fact. GPT-3's one hundred seventy-five billion is official. Context windows also vary by version. GPT-4 launched with eight thousand and thirty-two thousand token versions, and later variants went much higher. If you are not sure about a number, it is perfectly fine to say that detail is not public.
HOST: And Gemini? How does its design compare with other multimodal models?
EXPERT: The core idea is that Gemini was designed to be natively multimodal. Instead of attaching an image module to a text model, one model learns from text, images, and other inputs together. The benefits usually described are shared parameters across modalities, interactions between text and images during learning, data-efficient pre-training, and balanced training objectives. The broad idea is well established, but detailed claims about training stability compared with other companies' models are not fully published, so treat those with caution.
HOST: Let us zoom in on how text actually enters these models. What is tokenization?
EXPERT: LLMs do not read raw text. They read numbers. Tokenization splits text into smaller pieces called tokens, which can be whole words, parts of words, or single characters. Each token is mapped to a number, and the model works with those numbers. For example, the word tokenization might become token and ization.
HOST: Why not just use whole words?
EXPERT: The vocabulary would be enormous, and any new word would break the system. Splitting into sub-words keeps the vocabulary manageable, handles many languages, and copes with rare words. That improves both efficiency and performance.
HOST: What happens with a word the model has never seen, an out-of-vocabulary word?
EXPERT: Older systems replaced it with an unknown symbol and lost the meaning. Modern LLMs use sub-word methods like Byte-Pair Encoding and WordPiece. An unfamiliar word gets broken into smaller known pieces. Unhappiness might become un, happi, and ness. The model has seen all three, so it can work out the meaning from the parts. Some byte-level tokenizers can even fall back to individual bytes, so nothing is truly unknown.
HOST: Once we have tokens, what happens next?
EXPERT: Each token number is looked up in an embedding table and turned into a dense vector, which is just a list of numbers. These vectors capture meaning, so similar words end up close together in a high-dimensional space. That is the form neural networks can work with.
HOST: How are those embeddings set up in the beginning?
EXPERT: Often they are initialised randomly, and sometimes with pretrained vectors such as Word2Vec or GloVe. During training they are adjusted to capture what is useful for the task. In modern large language models, it is most common to start randomly and learn the embeddings together with the rest of the model. Pretrained vectors were more typical in older pipelines.
HOST: I keep hearing about context windows getting bigger. What is that?
EXPERT: The context window is how much text, measured in tokens, the model can look at in one go. That includes your input and the model's own output so far. If a conversation exceeds the limit, older parts must be cut off or summarised. You can picture a desk. The context window is how many papers fit on the desk at once. Anything not on the desk, the model cannot see.
HOST: So why not make it enormous?
EXPERT: Because the cost grows quickly with length. A larger window lets the model use more surrounding information and stay coherent over long documents, but it needs more memory and computation. So there is a trade-off between performance and resource efficiency.
HOST: Let us move to the architecture. Before transformers, there were sequence-to-sequence models. What are they?
EXPERT: A sequence-to-sequence model, often called Seq2Seq, turns one sequence into another, and the two lengths can differ. Machine translation is the classic case, where an English sentence goes in and a Hindi sentence comes out. Summarisation and speech recognition also fit. Traditionally there is an encoder that reads and compresses the input, and a decoder that produces the output step by step. Early versions used recurrent neural networks for both.
HOST: What was wrong with them that transformers fixed?
EXPERT: They were slow and forgetful. Recurrent models read one word at a time, so you could not parallelise training. And squeezing a whole sentence into one fixed-size summary created a bottleneck, so long sentences lost information.
HOST: And transformers solve that how?
EXPERT: In several ways. Self-attention processes all tokens at the same time, so training and inference are much faster on graphics processors. Any token can directly attend to any other, however far apart, so long-range relationships are captured well. Positional encoding supplies word order. They scale better to big datasets and long sequences. And the decoder can look at all of the encoder's outputs instead of one summary vector, so less context is lost.
HOST: You said encoder and decoder. How do they differ?
EXPERT: The encoder reads the input and builds rich internal representations. It is about understanding. The decoder takes those representations plus what it has already generated and produces the output one token at a time. It is about producing.
HOST: Does every model have both?
EXPERT: No. Encoder-only models like BERT are good for understanding tasks such as classification. Decoder-only models like the GPT family are built for generation, and most modern chat models are of this type. Encoder-decoder models like T5 suit translation and summarisation.
HOST: You mentioned positional encoding. Why is it needed?
EXPERT: Self-attention looks at all tokens at once and, on its own, has no sense of order. Dog bites man and man bites dog would look identical as a bag of words. Positional encodings inject the order. In the original transformer, a position signal is added to each word embedding, generated with sine and cosine functions of different frequencies. Many newer models use other schemes, such as rotary position embeddings or learned positions.
HOST: What about Mixture of Experts? I hear it in news about large models.
EXPERT: Imagine a hospital. You do not send every patient to every doctor. A receptionist sends you to the right specialist. In a Mixture of Experts model, the network has many expert sub-networks, and a gating function picks only a few of them for each token.
HOST: What is the benefit?
EXPERT: Lower computation per token, because only part of the model is active at a time, and much more total capacity. You can build a model with a huge number of parameters without paying the full cost of running all of them every time. The catches are that you still need memory to hold every expert, and the gate must balance the load so some experts are not overused.
HOST: Now the part everyone calls the heart of transformers. Explain attention in simple terms.
EXPERT: Attention lets the model decide which parts of the input matter most for the word it is currently working on, regardless of position. Instead of treating every token equally, it learns to pay attention to the relevant ones. Take the sentence, the dog chased the ball because it was fast. Attention helps the model connect the word it with an earlier word using context. And notice that even humans could debate whether it means the dog or the ball, which shows why context matters so much.
HOST: How is it actually computed?
EXPERT: The formula is attention equals softmax of the query matrix times the transpose of the key matrix, divided by the square root of the key dimension, and the result is multiplied by the value matrix. Every token produces three vectors. A query, which is what it is looking for. A key, which is what it contains. And a value, which is the information it offers.
HOST: And the intuition?
EXPERT: Think of a library. Your query is what you ask the librarian. The keys are the labels on books. The scores tell you which labels match. The values are the contents you read, weighted by how well they matched. The dot product between queries and keys measures similarity. We divide by the square root of the key dimension so the numbers do not become too large, which keeps the training stable. Softmax then turns the scores into weights that add up to one.
HOST: You said the dot product compares everything with everything. Is that expensive?
EXPERT: Yes. Every token is compared with every other token, so for a sequence of length n the cost grows with n squared. That is called quadratic complexity. Double the length and the attention cost roughly quadruples. That is why long contexts are expensive, and why researchers developed sparse attention, linear approximations, and optimised implementations like FlashAttention.
HOST: Why do transformers use multiple heads?
EXPERT: One attention pass can capture only one kind of relationship at a time. Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel, each with its own learned projections of queries, keys, and values. One head might track grammar, another might track what a pronoun refers to, another might focus on nearby words. Their outputs are combined into the final result. It is like a team of reviewers reading the same text with different focus, then merging their notes.
HOST: You keep mentioning softmax. Can you explain it properly?
EXPERT: We have a list of raw scores, which can be any number, even negative. We want probabilities, meaning positive numbers that add up to one. Step one, apply the exponential function to each score, which makes everything positive and boosts the larger scores. Step two, divide each by the sum of all of them. In words, softmax of one score equals e to the power of that score divided by the sum of e to the power of every score.
HOST: And its job in attention?
EXPERT: It converts raw similarity scores into attention weights, so the model can say give seventy percent of focus to this token, twenty percent to that one, and ten percent to the rest. In real implementations, the largest score is subtracted first for numerical stability. It does not change the result, but it prevents overflow.
HOST: Now the maths side. I know learners find it intimidating. Start with the chain rule.
EXPERT: The chain rule is how you find the derivative of a function inside another function. If you have f of g of x, the derivative is the derivative of f at g of x, times the derivative of g at x. You multiply the rates of change along the chain.
HOST: How does that relate to training a network?
EXPERT: A neural network is a long chain of functions, layer after layer, ending in a loss. To improve the model we need to know how the loss changes when each weight changes. The chain rule lets us compute that layer by layer, working backwards from the loss. That is backpropagation. Gradient descent then nudges each weight a little in the direction that reduces the loss. So backpropagation is essentially the chain rule applied efficiently.
HOST: What about the gradient with respect to the embeddings?
EXPERT: Embeddings are just parameters, so they get gradients like any other weight. By the chain rule, the gradient of the loss with respect to an embedding is the gradient of the loss with respect to the output scores, called logits, times the gradient of the logits with respect to the embedding. In practice the signal flows back through every layer in between, and the optimiser then updates the embedding vector. Frameworks like PyTorch do this automatically.
HOST: Where does the Jacobian matrix come in? That word sounds scary.
EXPERT: A Jacobian is just a table of derivatives. If a layer takes a vector in and produces a vector out, the Jacobian records how each output number changes with respect to each input number. Layers in a transformer, like attention and feed-forward blocks, are vector-to-vector functions, so the chain rule multiplies Jacobians to carry gradients backwards. In practice, frameworks never build those giant matrices. They compute efficient products of vectors with Jacobians, which is what reverse-mode automatic differentiation does.
HOST: Now the loss itself. Why is cross-entropy used for language modelling?
EXPERT: A language model outputs a probability for every token in the vocabulary, and the correct next token is known. Cross-entropy measures how far the predicted distribution is from the true one. With a single correct token, it simplifies to the negative logarithm of the probability the model gave to the correct token.
HOST: So a confident wrong answer is punished?
EXPERT: Yes. If the model gave the right word a high probability, the loss is small. If it gave it a tiny probability, the loss is large. It pushes the model to put probability on the right token, pairs nicely with softmax to give clean gradients, and directly maximises the likelihood of the training data. A related metric, perplexity, is just the exponential of the average cross-entropy.
HOST: And KL divergence? How is it different?
EXPERT: Kullback-Leibler divergence measures how one probability distribution differs from another. If P is the true distribution and Q is the model's, it is the sum over outcomes of P times the logarithm of P divided by Q. Zero means they match, and larger values mean they differ more. Cross-entropy equals the entropy of the true distribution plus the KL divergence, so minimising cross-entropy minimises KL.
HOST: Where do we see it in LLMs?
EXPERT: In knowledge distillation, where we compare student and teacher outputs, and in reinforcement learning from human feedback, where a KL penalty keeps the tuned model from drifting too far from the original. Remember it is not symmetric, and it is one tool among many, not a complete measure of text quality.
HOST: Let us cover eigenvalues and eigenvectors too, since they come up in dimensionality reduction.
EXPERT: For a matrix A, an eigenvector is a direction that does not change when A is applied. It only gets stretched or shrunk, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue. In Principal Component Analysis, you take the covariance matrix of your data. Its eigenvectors are the principal components, the directions along which the data varies most, and the eigenvalues tell you how much variance each one captures. You keep the components with the largest eigenvalues and drop the rest, which shrinks the data while preserving most of its information. Picture a tilted pancake of points in three dimensions. Two directions capture almost everything, and PCA finds that plane.
HOST: Next, activation functions. Walk us through the derivative of ReLU.
EXPERT: ReLU, the Rectified Linear Unit, is f of x equals the maximum of zero and x. If x is positive, the function is just x, so the slope is one. If x is negative, the output is zero, so the slope is zero. Exactly at zero it is not differentiable, but frameworks simply pick a value and it almost never matters.
HOST: Why does ReLU matter?
EXPERT: It adds non-linearity, which networks need to learn complex patterns. It is very cheap to compute. And for positive inputs the gradient is exactly one, which eases the vanishing gradient problem compared with sigmoid or tanh. The downside is dying ReLU, where a neuron stuck in the negative region outputs zero and stops learning. Many modern LLMs use smoother variants like GELU or SwiGLU in their feed-forward layers.
HOST: You mentioned vanishing gradients twice. What is it exactly?
EXPERT: During backpropagation, gradients are multiplied layer after layer. If those numbers are less than one, the gradient shrinks as it travels backwards, and early layers barely learn. It was especially bad in recurrent networks over long sequences.
HOST: How do transformers address it?
EXPERT: Three main things. Self-attention gives short, direct paths between any two tokens. Residual connections, also called skip connections, let gradients flow straight through the network. And layer normalisation keeps activations and gradients at a stable scale, which helps against both vanishing and exploding gradients.
HOST: Let us talk about how these models are trained. What is masked language modelling?
EXPERT: In masked language modelling, you hide some random tokens, say fifteen percent, with a mask symbol and train the model to predict them from the surrounding words. For example, the cat sat on the mask. The model must guess mat using both the left and right context. This forces it to learn how words relate to each other, building a deep understanding of language before any task-specific fine-tuning. BERT is the famous example.
HOST: BERT also had a second training task, right?
EXPERT: Yes, next sentence prediction. The model gets two sentences. Half the time the second really follows the first, and half the time it is random. The model classifies whether they belong together. The goal was to teach relationships between sentences, which matter for question answering and dialogue. Later research, such as RoBERTa, found it added little, so many newer models dropped it.
HOST: How do autoregressive models differ from masked ones?
EXPERT: Autoregressive models, like GPT, generate text one token at a time from left to right, each token predicted from the previous ones. They can only look backwards. Masked models, like BERT, fill in hidden tokens using context from both sides. Autoregressive models are natural for generation, because generation itself is left to right. Masked models are strong at understanding and classification. That is why chat assistants are autoregressive, while many classifiers and search encoders use masked-style models.
HOST: People also talk about hyperparameters. What are they?
EXPERT: Parameters are learned by the model during training, like weights. Hyperparameters are set by you before training and control how training happens. Examples are the learning rate, batch size, number of epochs, the choice of optimiser such as Adam, the choice of activation function, and the train-test split. Parameters are what the model learns, and hyperparameters are the dials you set before it starts.
HOST: What about overfitting? Every student hears about it.
EXPERT: Overfitting is when a model does great on training data but poorly on new data, because it memorised noise and quirks instead of learning general patterns. It is like a student who memorises last year's answer sheet and then fails when the questions change.
HOST: How do we prevent it?
EXPERT: Regularisation, which adds a penalty to the loss, using L1 or L2 methods. Dropout, which randomly switches off neurons during training. Data augmentation, which adds variety to the training set. Early stopping, which halts training when validation loss stops improving. And simpler models, with fewer parameters or layers. More data also helps.
HOST: There is a technique called adaptive softmax. What problem does it solve?
EXPERT: At the output layer, the model computes a probability for every word in the vocabulary, which can be hundreds of thousands of words. That is expensive. Adaptive softmax exploits the fact that a few words are very common and most are rare. It groups words into frequency-based clusters. Frequent words get the full, precise computation, and rare words go through smaller, cheaper computations. Since rare words are rare, you save a lot of work overall with very little loss of accuracy.
HOST: Fine-tuning a huge model sounds expensive. How do people do it on normal hardware?
EXPERT: With techniques like LoRA and QLoRA. LoRA stands for Low-Rank Adaptation. It freezes the original weights and adds small trainable adapter matrices to certain layers. These are low rank, meaning each is made of two thin matrices, so they hold only a tiny fraction of the parameters. You train just those.
HOST: So the base model stays the same?
EXPERT: Right, and that saves a lot of graphics memory. The adapters are small, and after training they can be merged back into the base weights, so inference cost does not increase. QLoRA goes one step further. It quantises the frozen base model to four-bit precision, shrinking memory dramatically, and trains LoRA adapters on top. It uses a special four-bit format called NormalFloat, double quantisation that compresses the quantisation constants too, and paged optimisers that handle memory spikes. The result is fine-tuning of very large models on a single graphics card, with quality close to full fine-tuning.
HOST: LoRA is a form of parameter-efficient fine-tuning, right?
EXPERT: Yes. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning, or PEFT, is the family of methods, including LoRA, adapters, and prompt tuning, that update only a small set of parameters and keep most of the model frozen. Because the original weights stay untouched, the model's core abilities are much less likely to be overwritten while it learns a new task.
HOST: So it reduces forgetting rather than removes it?
EXPERT: Good point. PEFT lowers the risk, but the adapted model can still change behaviour on other tasks. And as a bonus, you can keep one base model and swap in different small adapters for different tasks.
HOST: Let us define catastrophic forgetting properly, and how to fight it.
EXPERT: Catastrophic forgetting is when a model learns a new task and loses what it knew about earlier ones, because the weights that mattered for the old task get overwritten. Imagine learning Spanish so intensely that you forget your French. There are three main families of fixes. Rehearsal, where you mix some old data with the new data so the model keeps practising old skills. Elastic Weight Consolidation, where you work out which weights mattered for earlier tasks and penalise changing them too much. And modular approaches, like progressive neural networks, which add new modules for new tasks and leave old ones intact. In practice, parameter-efficient methods and low learning rates are widely used to limit forgetting.
HOST: What about making models smaller? What is distillation?
EXPERT: A large, powerful teacher model trains a smaller student model to imitate it. The key trick is that the student learns from the teacher's soft predictions, the full probability distributions, rather than just the single correct label. If the teacher says a picture is seventy percent cat, twenty-five percent lynx, and five percent dog, the student learns that cats and lynxes are similar. That extra information helps a small model perform well. The outcome is lower memory and computation with similar performance, which is ideal for phones and edge devices. With LLMs, distillation can also mean training a student on text generated by a stronger model.
HOST: Now, how does a model choose each next word? What is greedy decoding versus beam search?
EXPERT: Greedy decoding always picks the single most probable token at each step. It is fast, but short-sighted, because a locally best word can lead to a poor sentence overall. Beam search keeps several candidate sequences alive at once. With a beam width of five, it tracks the top five partial sentences, expands each, and keeps the best five overall. At the end it picks the best complete sequence.
HOST: Does that always give better text?
EXPERT: For tasks like translation and summarisation, yes, it tends to give more coherent output. For open-ended chat or creative writing, beam search can produce bland, repetitive text, which is why those use sampling methods instead.
HOST: Which brings us to temperature.
EXPERT: Temperature controls how random the output is, by reshaping the probability distribution before a token is chosen. Technically, it divides the logits before softmax. Low temperature, close to zero, sharpens the distribution, so the model almost always picks the most probable token and the output is focused and predictable. High temperature, above one, flattens it, so less likely tokens get a real chance, producing more varied, creative, and sometimes less sensible text. Around zero point seven is a common balance. Use lower values for factual answers and code, and higher values for brainstorming and stories.
HOST: And top-k versus top-p sampling?
EXPERT: Both sample only from plausible tokens so the model does not pick absurd ones. Top-k keeps only the k most probable tokens, say ten, and samples among them. It is simple, but k is fixed regardless of the situation. Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest set of tokens whose combined probability reaches a threshold such as zero point nine, and samples from that.
HOST: So top-p adapts?
EXPERT: Exactly. When the model is confident, two or three tokens may be enough. When it is uncertain, the set grows. That adaptiveness is the key difference.
HOST: Let us switch to using models. How does prompt engineering influence output?
EXPERT: LLMs are sensitive to wording. Prompt engineering is designing the input so the model behaves the way you want. Adding context, a clear task, a role, the output format, and constraints can noticeably improve accuracy and relevance. Saying summarise this gives a vague result. Saying you are a financial analyst, summarise this report in three bullet points for a busy executive, focusing on risks, gives a far better one.
HOST: And chain-of-thought prompting?
EXPERT: Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to work through a problem in intermediate steps before giving the final answer. For multi-step maths, logic, or planning, this usually improves accuracy because the model breaks the problem down instead of jumping to a guess. You can trigger it by adding an instruction like let us think step by step, or by giving examples with worked reasoning. A side benefit is interpretability, since you can read the steps, although the written reasoning is not always a faithful picture of what happens inside the model.
HOST: What is zero-shot learning?
EXPERT: Zero examples. In zero-shot use, you give the model only an instruction, with no demonstrations, and it performs the task using what it learned during pre-training. For example, classify this review as positive or negative works without any labelled training set. That shows how well large pre-trained models generalise across tasks like classification, translation, and summarisation.
HOST: And few-shot?
EXPERT: In few-shot prompting, you include a handful of examples in the prompt, maybe two to five, so the model sees the pattern. For instance, you show three emails with the correct category, then ask it to label a new one. The advantages are reduced data needs, flexibility because you can switch tasks just by changing the prompt, and cost efficiency because there is no new training run.
HOST: How should a developer choose between zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning?
EXPERT: Order them by effort and cost. Zero-shot is instruction only. Few-shot is instruction plus a few examples. Fine-tuning actually updates the model's weights. Start with the simplest approach that works, and move up only when quality demands it.
HOST: LLMs do not know my private documents. How do we fix that?
EXPERT: With Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG. Instead of relying only on what the model memorised, you fetch relevant information at question time and hand it to the model. The query is converted into an embedding and compared with pre-computed document embeddings to find relevant passages. Those passages are ranked by relevance. The top ones go into the prompt as context, so the model produces a grounded answer.
HOST: What has to happen before any of that?
EXPERT: An offline preparation step. You split documents into chunks, create embeddings, and store them in a vector database. Without that, there is nothing to retrieve. RAG reduces hallucinations, lets you use private or up-to-date data without retraining, and allows you to cite sources.
HOST: And knowledge graphs? How do they help?
EXPERT: A knowledge graph stores facts as entities and relationships, like Marie Curie won the Nobel Prize in Physics. It is structured and explicit, unlike the fuzzy knowledge inside an LLM. Combining them improves factual accuracy, because the model can check claims against the graph. It supports better reasoning, for multi-step questions like which company did the founder of a firm later start. And it gives richer context. Common uses include question answering, entity recognition, and recommendation systems.
HOST: Is that the same as RAG?
EXPERT: Related. Retrieving from a knowledge graph to feed the model is often called GraphRAG, and it is especially good for questions that require connecting several facts.
HOST: Now a practical scenario. Suppose your model suddenly starts producing offensive or factually wrong outputs. How do you diagnose it?
EXPERT: Follow a process. First, reproduce and analyse. Collect failing examples and look for patterns, such as certain topics or prompt styles. Second, check the inputs. Are prompts or retrieved documents causing it? Is there prompt injection, or did the system prompt change? Third, look at the data. Are there biases, gaps, or imbalances in the training or fine-tuning data, or errors in preprocessing? Fourth, review the model setup, including recent fine-tuning, hyperparameters, and decoding settings like a high temperature.
HOST: And the fix?
EXPERT: It depends on the cause. It might be retraining on more balanced data, data augmentation, debiasing, adversarial training, or better prompts. For factual errors, adding RAG often helps. Add output filters and safety classifiers as guardrails. Finally, measure and monitor. Build a test set from the failures, evaluate before and after, and keep monitoring in production.
HOST: Last question. What are the common challenges of using LLMs?
EXPERT: Five big ones. Computational resources, because training and serving need lots of graphics power and memory. Bias and fairness, because models can reproduce biases in their training data. Interpretability, because it is hard to explain why a model produced an answer. Data privacy, because large datasets raise privacy and security concerns. And cost, because development, training, and deployment are expensive, which can limit smaller organisations.
HOST: Anything you would add?
EXPERT: Hallucination, meaning confident but wrong answers. Prompt injection and other security risks. Latency, especially with long contexts. And evaluation difficulty, because open-ended outputs often have no single right answer.
HOST: If listeners remember one thing from today, what should it be?
EXPERT: Do not just memorise definitions. For every concept, ask what problem it solves, how it works in simple terms, and what the trade-off is. If you can answer those three, you can handle almost any follow-up question.

EXPERT: Thank you. Happy learning!
